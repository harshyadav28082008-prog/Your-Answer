Matter & Fluids

Explore how matter responds to forces, how pressure behaves in fluids, and why objects float, sink, stretch, compress, and flow.

Understanding matter

Matter is made of particles, and the arrangement and motion of those particles help explain the properties of solids, liquids, and gases.

Solid Particles are strongly bound and generally remain in relatively fixed positions.
Liquid Particles can move relative to one another while remaining close together.
Gas Particles are much more widely separated and move freely through the available space.

Density

Density describes how much mass is contained within a given volume. It is useful when comparing different materials and understanding whether an object is likely to float in a fluid.

Density

ρ = m / V

Density equals mass divided by volume.

Pressure

Pressure describes how much force acts perpendicular to a surface per unit area.

Pressure

P = F / A

A smaller area produces greater pressure for the same force.

Pressure is particularly important in fluids because a fluid at rest exerts pressure in all directions.

Pressure in fluids

In a fluid at rest, pressure generally increases with depth because the fluid above contributes to the force acting on lower layers.

Fluid pressure

P = P₀ + ρgh

P₀ is the pressure at the reference surface, ρ is fluid density, g is gravitational field strength, and h is depth.

Pascal's principle

When pressure is applied to an enclosed fluid, the pressure change is transmitted throughout the fluid.

This principle is used in hydraulic systems, where a force applied to one piston can produce a larger force at another piston with a greater area.

Hydraulic relationship

F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂

The pressure is equal throughout an ideal enclosed fluid.

Buoyancy

An object placed in a fluid experiences an upward force called buoyant force. This happens because fluid pressure is greater at greater depth.

Archimedes' principle gives a useful way to calculate this force.

Buoyant force

Fᵦ = ρVg

The buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid.

Elasticity

An elastic material can return toward its original shape after the deforming force is removed, provided the deformation stays within the material's elastic limit.

Springs provide a simple model for studying elastic behaviour.

Hooke's law

F = −kx

k is the spring constant and x is displacement from the equilibrium position. The negative sign represents the restoring direction.

Viscosity

Viscosity describes a fluid's resistance to flow. A highly viscous fluid flows less easily than a fluid with low viscosity.

Viscosity becomes especially important when studying real fluid motion, where friction within the fluid cannot be ignored.

Why do objects float?

Whether an object floats depends on the relationship between its weight and the buoyant force acting on it.

Floats The buoyant force can balance the object's weight before the object becomes completely submerged.
Sinks The object's weight is greater than the available buoyant force.
Neutral buoyancy The buoyant force and weight are equal while the object remains submerged.

Quick example

Apply the idea

A material has a mass of 200 g and occupies a volume of 100 cm³. What is its density?

Using:

ρ = m / V

ρ = 200 / 100

Answer: The density is 2 g/cm³.
