Electricity

Explore electric charge, current, potential difference, resistance, circuits, electrical power, and the movement of electrical energy through systems.

Electric charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter. There are two types of charge: positive and negative.

Like charges Charges of the same sign repel one another.
Unlike charges Charges of opposite signs attract one another.
Charge conservation Electric charge cannot simply be created or destroyed; it is conserved in an isolated system.

Coulomb's law

The electric force between two point charges depends on the magnitude of the charges and the distance separating them.

Electric force

F = k|q₁q₂| / r²

k is Coulomb's constant, q₁ and q₂ are the charges, and r is their separation.

Electric current

Electric current measures the rate at which electric charge passes through a cross-section of a conductor.

Current

I = Q / t

I is current, Q is charge transferred, and t is time.

Current is measured in amperes (A), where one ampere corresponds to one coulomb of charge passing a point each second.

Potential difference

Potential difference describes the energy transferred per unit charge between two points in an electrical system.

Potential difference

V = W / Q

V is potential difference, W is energy transferred, and Q is charge.

Resistance

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes the flow of electric current.

Ohm's law

V = IR

For an ohmic conductor under suitable conditions, voltage is proportional to current.

Electric circuits

A circuit provides a complete path through which charge can move. Components connected in different arrangements behave differently.

Series circuit Components share the same current. The total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances for ideal resistors.
Parallel circuit Components share the same potential difference across parallel branches, while the current divides between the branches.

Kirchhoff's laws

Kirchhoff's laws provide powerful tools for analysing more complicated electrical circuits.

Current law The total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it.
Voltage law The algebraic sum of potential changes around a complete circuit loop is zero.

Electrical power

Electrical power describes the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.

Electrical power

P = VI

Using Ohm's law, this can also be written as P = I²R or P = V²/R for suitable resistive circuits.

Electrical energy

If electrical power remains constant for a period of time, the energy transferred can be calculated directly.

Electrical energy

E = Pt

E is energy transferred, P is power, and t is time.

Quick example

Apply the idea

A resistor has a resistance of 6 Ω and carries a current of 2 A. What potential difference is across it?

Using Ohm's law:

V = IR

V = 2 × 6

Answer: The potential difference is 12 V.
