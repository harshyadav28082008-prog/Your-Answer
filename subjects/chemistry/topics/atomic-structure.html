Atomic Structure

Everything in chemistry is built from atoms. Understanding their internal structure explains the behaviour of elements, the arrangement of electrons, and ultimately how atoms interact with one another.

1. Subatomic Particles

An atom contains three fundamental particles that are important in introductory chemistry: protons, neutrons, and electrons.

p⁺

Proton

Positively charged particle found inside the nucleus.

n⁰

Neutron

Electrically neutral particle found inside the nucleus.

e⁻

Electron

Negatively charged particle found in regions around the nucleus.

2. Atomic Number and Mass Number

The identity of an element is determined by its number of protons. This number is called the atomic number.

Atomic number

Z = number of protons

The mass number represents the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Mass number

A = Z + N

Here, A is the mass number, Z is the atomic number, and N is the number of neutrons.

3. Development of Atomic Models

Our understanding of the atom developed through several models. Each model improved upon limitations of earlier ideas.

Dalton's model

Matter was described as being composed of indivisible atoms.

Thomson's model

The discovery of the electron showed that atoms contained smaller charged particles.

Rutherford's model

Scattering experiments provided evidence for a small, positively charged nucleus.

Bohr's model

Electrons were described as occupying specific energy levels.

Quantum mechanical model

Modern chemistry describes electrons using probability distributions called orbitals rather than fixed circular paths.

4. Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that contain the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Example: Carbon

Carbon-12 and Carbon-14 both contain six protons.

Carbon-12 has six neutrons, while Carbon-14 has eight.

They therefore have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

5. Electronic Configuration

Electrons occupy regions around the nucleus according to their energy. These regions are organised into shells and subshells.

The four commonly introduced subshell types are s, p, d, and f.

Maximum electrons

The s subshell can contain 2 electrons, p can contain 6, d can contain 10, and f can contain 14.

Subshell capacities

s²    p⁶    d¹⁰    f¹⁴

6. Quantum Numbers

Quantum numbers describe the state of an electron in an atom. Four quantum numbers are used to describe an electron.

Principal quantum number (n)

Indicates the main energy level or shell.

Azimuthal quantum number (ℓ)

Describes the subshell and orbital shape.

Magnetic quantum number (mℓ)

Describes the orientation of an orbital.

Spin quantum number (mₛ)

Describes the spin state of an electron.

7. Rules of Electronic Arrangement

Three important principles help determine how electrons are arranged in atoms.

Aufbau principle

Electrons generally occupy lower-energy orbitals before higher-energy orbitals.

Pauli exclusion principle

No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers.

Hund's rule

Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly before pairing, when possible.

Key Ideas

Atomic number identifies the element.

It equals the number of protons in the nucleus.

Isotopes differ in neutron number.

They belong to the same element but have different mass numbers.

Electrons determine chemical behaviour.

Particularly important are the electrons in the outermost occupied energy levels.
