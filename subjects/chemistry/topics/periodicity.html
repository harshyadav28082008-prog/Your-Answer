Periodic Table & Periodicity

The periodic table is more than a list of elements. Its structure reveals repeating patterns in atomic structure and helps us predict how elements behave.

1. The Periodic Law

When elements are arranged according to increasing atomic number, their physical and chemical properties show repeating patterns. This is the basis of the modern periodic table.

Modern periodic law

The properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

2. Groups and Periods

The periodic table is organised into horizontal rows called periods and vertical columns called groups.

Periods

There are seven periods. Elements within a period have the same number of occupied principal electron shells.

Groups

Elements in the same group often have similar chemical properties because of related valence-electron arrangements.

H He Li Be F Ne Na Mg Cl Ar

3. Blocks of the Periodic Table

Elements can also be classified according to the subshell into which their differentiating electron enters.

s-block

Includes groups 1 and 2, along with helium by electron configuration.

p-block

Includes groups 13 through 18.

d-block

Contains the transition elements.

f-block

Contains the lanthanides and actinides.

4. Atomic Radius

Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom. Its trend is strongly influenced by nuclear charge, electron shells, and shielding.

→ decreases

Across a period

Atomic radius generally decreases from left to right because effective nuclear attraction increases.

↓ increases

Down a group

Atomic radius generally increases because additional electron shells are added.

5. Ionisation Energy

Ionisation energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

↑ generally increases

Across a period

Increasing effective nuclear charge generally makes electrons harder to remove.

↓ generally decreases

Down a group

Outer electrons become farther from the nucleus and are generally easier to remove.

6. Electronegativity

Electronegativity describes the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electrons toward itself.

General trend

Electronegativity generally increases from left to right across a period and decreases down a group.

Fluorine has the highest electronegativity on the commonly used Pauling scale.

7. Electron Affinity

Electron affinity describes the energy change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom.

Its exact trend contains several exceptions because electron configurations and electron-electron repulsions also influence the energy change.

8. Metals, Nonmetals and Metalloids

Property Metals Nonmetals
Electrical conductivity Generally high Generally low
Appearance Often lustrous Often non-lustrous
Malleability Generally malleable Usually brittle when solid
Typical ion formation Often form cations Often form anions or share electrons

Metalloids display a mixture of metallic and nonmetallic properties and occur near the boundary between the two broad regions.

Key Ideas

Atomic number controls periodic position.

The modern table is arranged according to increasing atomic number.

Electron configuration explains many trends.

The arrangement of electrons helps explain why elements in particular groups behave similarly.

Periodic trends are patterns, not absolute rules.

General trends are useful for prediction, but individual electron configurations can produce exceptions.
