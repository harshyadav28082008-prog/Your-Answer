States of Matter

Why does ice keep its shape while water flows and steam spreads through a room? Explore how particle arrangement, motion, temperature and pressure determine the physical state of a substance.

1. What determines the state of matter?

Matter is made of particles that are constantly moving. The state of a substance depends on the balance between the particles' kinetic energy and the attractive forces between them.

Kinetic energy

Energy associated with motion. As temperature increases, the average kinetic energy of particles generally increases.

Intermolecular forces

Attractions between particles. Stronger attractions generally make it harder for particles to separate.

Core idea: A change in temperature or pressure can change how particles move and interact, causing a substance to change its state.

2. The three common states

🧊

Solid

Particles are closely packed and usually vibrate around fixed positions. Solids have a definite shape and volume.

💧

Liquid

Particles remain close together but can move past one another. Liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of their container.

☁️

Gas

Particles are far apart relative to their size and move freely. Gases expand to fill their containers and are easily compressed.

⚡

Plasma

Plasma is an ionised state containing charged particles. It occurs in stars, lightning and some specialised lamps.

Property Solid Liquid Gas
Shape Definite Takes container's shape Fills container
Volume Definite Definite Not definite
Compressibility Very low Low High
Particle movement Mainly vibration Particles slide past each other Free, rapid movement

3. Gas laws

Gas laws describe how pressure, volume and temperature are related for a fixed amount of gas. Unless stated otherwise, temperature in gas-law calculations must be measured in kelvin.

Boyle's law

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

At constant temperature and amount of gas, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

Charles's law

V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂

At constant pressure and amount of gas, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

Pressure law

P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂

At constant volume and amount of gas, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

Avogadro's law

V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂

At constant temperature and pressure, gas volume is directly proportional to the amount of gas in moles.

Using the laws correctly

  • Convert Celsius to kelvin: T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15.
  • Keep pressure and volume units consistent on both sides.
  • Identify which variables remain constant before choosing a law.

4. The ideal gas equation

Combining the gas laws gives a useful equation relating pressure, volume, temperature and the amount of gas.

Ideal gas equation

PV = nRT
  • P = pressure
  • V = volume
  • n = amount of gas in moles
  • R = universal gas constant
  • T = absolute temperature in kelvin

A commonly used value is R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when pressure is in pascals and volume is in cubic metres. Other values of R can be used with compatible units.

Remember: The units must match the chosen value of R. For example, using pressure in pascals and volume in litres without converting units will give an incorrect result.

5. Kinetic molecular theory

The kinetic molecular theory models gas particles to explain observable gas behaviour. In the ideal model:

  • Particles are treated as having negligible individual volume compared with the container.
  • Particles move continuously and randomly.
  • Collisions between particles and container walls are elastic.
  • There are no intermolecular attractions or repulsions except during collisions.
  • The average translational kinetic energy depends on absolute temperature.

Average translational kinetic energy

Ēₖ = 3RT/2

This is the average translational kinetic energy per mole of an ideal gas. For one particle, the expression is 3kT/2, where k is Boltzmann's constant.

6. Changes of state

A physical change of state alters the arrangement and motion of particles without changing the chemical identity of the substance.

Change Direction Heat transfer
Melting Solid → Liquid Absorbed
Freezing Liquid → Solid Released
Vaporisation Liquid → Gas Absorbed
Condensation Gas → Liquid Released
Sublimation Solid → Gas directly Absorbed
Deposition Gas → Solid directly Released

Latent heat

During a phase change at a given pressure, energy can be absorbed or released without a temperature change. This energy changes particle interactions rather than increasing average kinetic energy.

Heat during a phase change

q = mL

Here, q is heat transferred, m is mass and L is the specific latent heat for the relevant phase change.

7. Vapour pressure, evaporation and boiling

Evaporation

Evaporation occurs at the surface of a liquid when some particles have enough energy to escape into the gas phase. It can occur below the boiling point.

Vapour pressure

In a closed container, vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by vapour when it is in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid at a particular temperature.

Boiling point

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure. Therefore, a liquid's boiling point can change when external pressure changes.

Example: Water boils at a lower temperature at high altitude because atmospheric pressure is lower than at sea level.

8. Ideal gases versus real gases

An ideal gas is a simplified model. Real gas particles have finite volume and experience intermolecular forces, so real gases can deviate from the ideal gas equation.

Ideal gas Real gas
Particle volume is treated as negligible. Particles have finite volume.
Intermolecular forces are ignored. Intermolecular forces can matter.
Follows PV = nRT exactly in the model. May deviate from PV = nRT.
An approximation. Describes actual substances.

Real gases generally behave more like ideal gases at relatively low pressure and high temperature, when particles are farther apart and intermolecular attractions have less influence.

Compressibility factor

Z = PV / nRT

The compressibility factor Z measures deviation from ideal-gas behaviour. For an ideal gas, Z = 1.

9. Key ideas to remember

  • The physical state depends on particle motion and intermolecular attractions.
  • Solids have a definite shape and volume; liquids have a definite volume; gases fill their containers.
  • Use kelvin in gas-law calculations.
  • The ideal gas equation is PV = nRT.
  • During a phase change, heat transfer does not necessarily change temperature.
  • Boiling occurs when vapour pressure equals external pressure.
  • Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour, especially at high pressure and low temperature.
