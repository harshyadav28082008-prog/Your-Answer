Organic Chemistry Foundations

Carbon forms an extraordinary variety of compounds, from simple gases to complex biological molecules. Organic chemistry studies their structures, properties, preparation, and reactions. This lesson introduces the fundamental ideas needed to understand that remarkable chemical diversity.

1. What Is Organic Chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry concerned primarily with carbon compounds. It studies their structures, physical and chemical properties, preparation, and transformations.

Most organic compounds contain carbon and hydrogen. Many also contain oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, or halogens.

Important distinction

Not every carbon-containing compound is classified as organic. Carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, carbonates, bicarbonates, and some other simple carbon compounds are traditionally studied in inorganic chemistry.

Why is carbon so important?

Carbon can form stable bonds with itself and with many other elements. This allows it to produce chains, branches, rings, and complex three-dimensional structures.

2. Unique Nature of Carbon

2.1 Tetravalency

Carbon has atomic number 6 and electronic configuration 1s² 2s² 2p². It has four valence electrons and commonly forms four covalent bonds to complete its octet.

Carbon electronic configuration

1s² 2s² 2p²

This ability to form four covalent bonds is called tetravalency.

Example: Methane

Methane (CH₄) contains one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms.

CH₄

Each C–H bond is a shared pair of electrons. Carbon has four bonds in total.

2.2 Catenation

Catenation is the ability of an element to form covalent bonds with atoms of the same element, producing chains or rings.

Carbon exhibits extensive catenation because carbon-carbon covalent bonds are sufficiently stable to form a large variety of structures.

Common carbon skeletons

Straight chains: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

Branched chains: (CH₃)₃CH

Rings: cyclohexane (C₆H₁₂)

2.3 Multiple bonding

Carbon can form single, double, and triple bonds with other carbon atoms.

Single bond

Ethane: CH₃–CH₃

One shared electron pair.

Double bond

Ethene: CH₂=CH₂

Two shared electron pairs.

Triple bond

Ethyne: HC≡CH

Three shared electron pairs.

3. Hybridisation and Molecular Geometry

Hybridisation is a model in which atomic orbitals combine to form new equivalent hybrid orbitals. It helps explain the bonding and geometry of many organic molecules.

3.1 sp³ hybridisation

One s orbital and three p orbitals combine to form four sp³ hybrid orbitals.

Geometry

Tetrahedral · 109.5°

Example: methane (CH₄). Its carbon atom forms four sigma bonds.

3.2 sp² hybridisation

One s orbital and two p orbitals combine to form three sp² hybrid orbitals. One unhybridised p orbital remains.

Geometry

Trigonal planar · 120°

Example: ethene (C₂H₄). The remaining p orbitals overlap sideways to form a pi bond.

3.3 sp hybridisation

One s orbital and one p orbital combine to form two sp hybrid orbitals. Two unhybridised p orbitals remain.

Geometry

Linear · 180°

Example: ethyne (C₂H₂), which contains one sigma bond and two pi bonds between its carbon atoms.

Hybridisation Geometry Angle Example
sp³ Tetrahedral 109.5° CH₄
sp² Trigonal planar 120° C₂H₄
sp Linear 180° C₂H₂

4. Classification of Organic Compounds

Organic compounds can be classified according to the arrangement of their carbon atoms and the functional groups they contain.

4.1 Acyclic or open-chain compounds

These compounds contain carbon atoms arranged in open chains. The chains may be straight or branched.

Examples

Butane: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

Isobutane: (CH₃)₃CH

4.2 Cyclic compounds

These compounds contain atoms connected in closed rings.

Homocyclic compounds

The ring consists only of carbon atoms. Examples include cyclopropane and benzene.

Heterocyclic compounds

The ring contains carbon and at least one other element, such as nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur. Examples include pyridine and furan.

4.3 Saturated and unsaturated compounds

Saturated compounds contain only carbon-carbon single bonds in their carbon skeleton.

Unsaturated compounds contain at least one carbon-carbon double or triple bond.

Alkanes

Contain carbon-carbon single bonds.

Example: Ethane (C₂H₆).

Alkenes

Contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

Example: Ethene (C₂H₄).

Alkynes

Contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.

Example: Ethyne (C₂H₂).

5. Representing Organic Compounds

Organic compounds can be represented in several ways. Each representation highlights different structural information.

5.1 Molecular formula

A molecular formula tells us the number of atoms of each element in one molecule.

Example

Ethanol: C₂H₆O

5.2 Condensed structural formula

This representation shows how atoms or groups are arranged without drawing every bond.

Example

Ethanol: CH₃CH₂OH

5.3 Expanded structural formula

This representation shows the bonds between individual atoms more explicitly.

It is useful when examining connectivity, valency, and functional groups.

5.4 Skeletal formula

In skeletal representations, carbon atoms are usually implied at line ends and corners. Hydrogen atoms attached to carbon are omitted and understood from carbon's usual valency.

Remember

The molecular formula alone may not identify a unique compound. Different structures can share the same molecular formula.

6. Functional Groups

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms that gives an organic compound characteristic chemical properties and reactions.

Functional groups help us classify organic compounds and understand their chemical behaviour.

7. Homologous Series

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds with the same functional group and general formula, in which successive members differ by one –CH₂– unit.

Difference between successive members

–CH₂– = 14 u

Members of a homologous series generally show similar chemical properties. Their physical properties usually change gradually with increasing molecular size.

Example: Alkane series

8. Basic IUPAC Nomenclature

IUPAC nomenclature provides a systematic method of naming chemical compounds so that their structures can be identified consistently.

8.1 Root words

The root word indicates the number of carbon atoms in the selected parent chain.

Carbon atoms Root
1Meth-
2Eth-
3Prop-
4But-
5Pent-
6Hex-
7Hept-
8Oct-
9Non-
10Dec-

8.2 Primary suffixes

Bond type Suffix
Single -ane
Double -ene
Triple -yne

8.3 A basic naming procedure

  1. Identify the principal characteristic functional group.
  2. Select the parent chain containing that group and the maximum appropriate number of multiple bonds.
  3. Number the parent chain according to IUPAC priority rules.
  4. Identify substituents and their positions.
  5. Arrange prefixes alphabetically where required.
  6. Use the appropriate suffix and assemble the complete name.

Example 1: CH₃–CH₂–CH₃

The longest chain contains three carbon atoms.

All carbon-carbon bonds are single bonds.

Root = Prop-

Suffix = -ane

IUPAC name: Propane

Example 2: CH₃–CH=CH₂

The parent chain contains three carbon atoms and one double bond.

The double bond receives the lowest possible locant, which is 1.

IUPAC name: Prop-1-ene

Example 3: CH₃–CH₂–OH

The compound contains two carbon atoms and an alcohol functional group.

Root = Eth-

Functional group suffix = -ol

IUPAC name: Ethanol

9. Isomerism

Isomers are compounds that have the same molecular formula but differ in the arrangement of their atoms or in their spatial arrangement.

Isomerism is important because different structures can have different physical, chemical, and biological properties.

9.1 Structural isomerism

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different connectivity between atoms.

Chain isomerism

Different arrangements of the carbon skeleton.

Example: Butane and 2-methylpropane.

Position isomerism

The same functional group or multiple bond occurs at different positions.

Example: Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol.

Functional group isomerism

Compounds have the same molecular formula but different functional groups.

Example: Ethanol and dimethyl ether (C₂H₆O).

9.2 Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in the spatial arrangement of atoms.

Geometrical isomerism

Arises when restricted rotation, commonly around a carbon-carbon double bond, produces different spatial arrangements.

Example: cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene.

Optical isomerism

Often arises when a molecule is chiral, meaning it is not superimposable on its mirror image.

Such molecules can exist as enantiomers, which rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions.

10. Fundamental Organic Reactions

Organic reactions involve changes in chemical bonds and the arrangement of atoms. They can be classified according to the type of transformation taking place.

10.1 Substitution reaction

An atom or group in a molecule is replaced by another atom or group.

Example

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl

This reaction takes place under suitable photochemical conditions.

10.2 Addition reaction

Atoms or groups are added across a multiple bond, reducing its bond order.

Example: Hydrogenation

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃–CH₃

The reaction occurs in the presence of a suitable catalyst, such as nickel, under appropriate conditions.

10.3 Elimination reaction

Atoms or groups are removed from a molecule, often resulting in the formation of a multiple bond.

Example

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O

Ethanol can undergo dehydration under suitable conditions to form ethene.

10.4 Oxidation and reduction

In organic chemistry, oxidation often involves an increase in bonds to oxygen or a decrease in bonds to hydrogen. Reduction generally involves the opposite changes.

Example: Oxidation of a primary alcohol

A primary alcohol can be oxidised to an aldehyde and, under suitable conditions, further to a carboxylic acid.

11. Introduction to Electronic Effects

The distribution of electrons within an organic molecule influences its stability, reactivity, acidity, and basicity.

11.1 Inductive effect

The inductive effect is the permanent displacement of sigma-bond electron density due to differences in electronegativity between bonded atoms.

Electron-withdrawing groups show a negative inductive effect (−I), while electron-releasing groups show a positive inductive effect (+I).

11.2 Resonance effect

Resonance describes electron delocalisation across a system of overlapping p orbitals. A molecule may be represented by multiple contributing structures, while its actual electronic structure is a resonance hybrid.

Example: Benzene

Benzene has six delocalised pi electrons spread over its six-membered carbon ring. This delocalisation contributes to its unusual stability.

11.3 Hyperconjugation

Hyperconjugation is the delocalisation of electrons from a sigma bond into an adjacent suitable orbital or pi system.

It can influence the stability of carbocations, radicals, and substituted alkenes.

11.4 Electromeric effect

The electromeric effect is a temporary, complete transfer of pi electrons in a multiple bond under the influence of an attacking reagent.

Key Ideas

Carbon's versatility

Tetravalency, catenation, and multiple bonding allow carbon to form an enormous variety of compounds.

Functional groups

Functional groups help determine the characteristic reactions of organic compounds.

IUPAC nomenclature

Systematic naming allows organic structures to be identified clearly and consistently.

Isomerism

The same molecular formula can correspond to different structures and spatial arrangements.

Organic reactions

Substitution, addition, elimination, oxidation, and reduction describe important types of chemical transformations.
